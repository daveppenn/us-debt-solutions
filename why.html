The case for fiscal stability

Today's deficits.
Tomorrow's consequences.

The current national debt may be manageable. The concern is whether large deficits, repeated year after year, will eventually make it unmanageable.

1. Today's debt is not necessarily a crisis

~$40.6 trillion

Total federal debt outstanding, November 5, 2026; approximately $32.8T held by the public and $7.8T in intragovernmental holdings. Source: U.S. Treasury, Debt to the Penny.

That number sounds unimaginable, but it does not establish by itself that the debt is unmanageable. A household with a mortgage and a strong income may be in better financial shape than someone with a much smaller loan and no income. For a country, the relevant questions include the size of its economy, the cost of interest, its capacity to collect revenue, and whether lenders trust its currency and institutions.

The United States does not need to pay off every Treasury bond tomorrow. It needs a credible way to service obligations and keep debt from outgrowing its economic capacity.

A deficit is the amount the government borrows in one year. Debt is the accumulated stock. Running a deficit is not automatically dangerous—especially in a recession or when borrowing finances productive investments. But repeated large deficits during normal economic conditions add debt faster than the economy may be able to absorb comfortably.

In its February 2026 outlook, the Congressional Budget Office projected debt held by the public rising from about 101% of GDP in 2026 to 120% in 2036 under its baseline assumptions.

The question is not whether today's debt is already too large. It's what happens if we keep adding deficits at this pace.

3. What could happen if persistent deficits eventually create a crisis?

It may not begin with a dramatic default. The first signs could be much more ordinary—and more familiar.

Higher borrowing costs

Investors demand higher Treasury yields. Mortgages, business loans, and other rates may rise alongside them.

Less room to respond

More tax revenue goes to interest, leaving less flexibility for defense, infrastructure, research, or a future recession.

Inflation and weaker purchasing power

If policymakers rely on monetary financing or lose control of inflation expectations, money can buy less over time. Hyperinflation is an extreme possibility, not an inevitable or currently forecast outcome.

A confidence shock

Investors may suddenly demand much higher yields or shift away from dollar assets. Such shifts can disrupt credit markets even without a formal default.

There is no universally agreed debt threshold at which a crisis automatically begins. Growth, interest rates, policy credibility, and global demand for dollar assets all matter. The risk is that waiting for unmistakable evidence may mean waiting until the solution is far more expensive.

4. Why trustworthy money matters beyond finance

Money is more than paper or a bank balance. It is a common measuring stick for the value of work, savings, ideas, and investment. A currency people trust lets strangers cooperate, businesses plan years ahead, lenders finance new ventures, and workers save for the future.

Consider an inventor with a better battery, a company developing a medical treatment, or a contractor rebuilding a bridge. Each needs people willing to commit labor and capital today in exchange for money they expect to retain value tomorrow.

Sound, widely accepted money helps turn the incentive to solve problems into the ability to finance solutions.

When people lose confidence in a currency, the incentives do not disappear completely. But they can become distorted: people devote more energy to protecting purchasing power, moving capital, or speculating on prices, and less to long-term investment and productive risk-taking.

That does not mean capitalism instantly breaks down when government debt rises. Markets can function with substantial public debt, and innovation depends on far more than monetary stability. The narrower, stronger point is that stable money and credible public finances support the trust that productive markets need.

5. What do we owe our children?

Every generation inherits both the achievements and the obligations of the generations before it. Public borrowing can be justified when it builds things that future Americans benefit from—safer infrastructure, scientific discoveries, or a stronger economy. But borrowing year after year simply to avoid difficult choices today raises a moral question: is it fair to leave the bill, and the risk, to people who have no voice in the decision?

Our children will face challenges we cannot predict: medical breakthroughs to fund, new technologies to develop, natural disasters, national-security threats, and problems no one has imagined yet. A government already constrained by high interest costs may have fewer options to help them meet those challenges.

We should leave the next generation more opportunities—not fewer choices because we were unwilling to make our own.

This is not an argument that every deficit is immoral. Future generations can benefit from wise investments financed with debt, and an economy that grows can carry substantial obligations. The responsibility is to distinguish investments that create lasting value from a pattern of borrowing that shifts today's costs and risks into tomorrow.

Fiscal responsibility is also an act of stewardship: preserving the financial flexibility, trusted currency, and economic opportunity that our children will need to solve problems of their own.

6. The goal is prevention, not panic

Fiscal repair becomes harder when undertaken during a crisis. Higher interest costs can compound the problem, while sudden spending cuts or tax increases can weaken an already fragile economy.

A credible plan should therefore be gradual, measurable, and sensitive to recessions. It should preserve every contractual Treasury principal and interest obligation: reducing those payments could be interpreted as default and drive borrowing rates higher, making the original problem worse.

The Emergency Debt Stabilization Surcharge proposes a structural deficit target of 2% of GDP, a phased adjustment to eligible federal payments and receipts, and recession safeguards. Congress could lower the required surcharge by enacting other durable spending reductions or revenue measures—including tariffs where they generate net additional revenue.

The objective is not zero debt. It is a sustainable path that protects the dollar, the credit system, and the ability of future generations to solve problems.
