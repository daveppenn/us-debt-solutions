The Emergency Debt Stabilization Surcharge

A rules-based framework for restoring U.S. fiscal sustainability

Executive summary

The immediate challenge is not simply the stock of U.S. federal debt but the persistent pace of borrowing. The Emergency Debt Stabilization Surcharge (EDSS) would target a structural federal deficit of 2% of GDP during normal economic conditions. It would reduce eligible noninterest federal payments and increase eligible federal receipts by a uniform percentage, with an automatic phase-out as the underlying fiscal position improves.

Unlike an immediate fiscal shock, the policy would phase in over 36 months by default, allowing Congress to enact spending reforms, increase tariff revenue, or adopt other revenue measures that lower the required surcharge.

How the mechanism works

Congress sets a 2%-of-GDP structural deficit target. The surcharge is determined by the projected gap between the baseline deficit and that target, divided by the sum of eligible noninterest spending and the eligible federal receipt base.

Surcharge rate = Required deficit reduction ÷ (eligible noninterest spending + eligible receipts)

Tariffs count as federal revenue and lower the deficit gap. The receipt base must be defined to avoid double counting. Any additional spending cuts or revenue increases already included in baseline figures must not be counted again.

Congress retains control over fiscal policy; the formula makes the cost of changes visible. The surcharge automatically falls to zero when the structural deficit reaches the target without it.

A 36-month phase-in

The surcharge would increase gradually rather than immediately applying the full adjustment. A 36-month linear phase-in is the default, with Congress able to establish a different statutory schedule.

At the same time, Congress could pursue durable deficit improvements through spending restraint, targeted revenue measures, or tariffs. Those measures would reduce the remaining surcharge required to achieve the target. Increases in tariffs should be evaluated net of potential economic and revenue offsets, not treated as costless.

For illustration, with no other reforms and unchanged economic conditions, a final 11.53% surcharge would phase in to roughly 3.84% after 12 months, 7.68% after 24 months, and 11.53% after 36 months. If other reforms reduced the deficit, the final rate would be lower. The 2% target would be reached only when the full required adjustment was in place.

Why Treasury principal and interest are protected

The U.S. government should honor Treasury securities in full. Arbitrarily reducing contractual interest or principal payments could be viewed as a partial default or restructuring. Investors would likely demand greater compensation for credit and legal risk, potentially raising borrowing costs on newly issued and refinanced Treasury debt.

Higher Treasury yields could weaken the value of outstanding securities, disrupt collateral and funding markets, and eventually raise annual debt-service costs as securities mature and are refinanced. That could create a self-defeating cycle: an attempted saving on interest increases future interest expense.

Reduce future borrowing—not confidence in existing Treasury obligations.

Interest is exempt from the surcharge. Treasury principal repayments and rollovers must also be honored under their contractual terms, though principal refinancing is not generally counted as ordinary federal budget spending.

Recession safeguard: target the structural deficit

During a recession, tax receipts typically fall while unemployment-related and other safety-net spending rises. Automatically increasing taxes and reducing benefits to offset these temporary changes could deepen the downturn.

The EDSS would therefore use a cyclically adjusted (structural) deficit for its normal target. A statutory recession trigger—such as a sustained unemployment increase under a Sahm Rule-style indicator, supported by broader economic data—would temporarily freeze or reduce scheduled surcharge increases.

Automatic stabilizers would remain operational, and temporary recession-related deficits would be permitted. Once recovery was established, the surcharge would resume a gradual path toward the structural target. Congress could also authorize temporary emergency measures through normal legislation.

Independent economic and budget projections would be published, with predefined thresholds to minimize political manipulation. A recession pause would not erase the underlying fiscal objective.

Implementation and safeguards

Congress would need to define eligible payment and receipt categories in statute. Existing federal contracts, benefits, compensation obligations, refunds, and intergovernmental transfers require careful legal treatment. The executive branch could not simply withhold legally mandated payments without congressional authorization.

Independent estimates from the Congressional Budget Office and relevant agencies would determine the structural deficit and publish the adjustment calculation. Rates could be reviewed quarterly, with caps on changes to avoid instability. One-time asset sales and accounting shifts should not masquerade as permanent deficit improvements.

A universal adjustment can burden low-income households disproportionately and disrupt service delivery. Congress should examine distributional effects and any targeted protections transparently, recognizing that exemptions require a higher rate elsewhere or alternative offsets.

Why 2% of GDP?

A balanced budget is not always required to stabilize the debt ratio. With sustained nominal GDP growth and a sufficiently small deficit relative to GDP, debt can grow more slowly than the economy. A 2% target is an intelligible fiscal anchor, though it does not guarantee sustainability under every growth and interest-rate scenario.

Conclusion

The goal is not to eliminate federal debt. It is to prevent persistent structural deficits from making that debt unmanageable. A transparent, phased, rules-based adjustment—combined with recession safeguards and full protection of Treasury obligations—deserves serious examination.
