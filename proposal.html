The 50/50 Deficit Reduction Plan

A rules-based framework for restoring U.S. fiscal sustainability

Executive summary

The 50/50 Plan aims to cut the annual federal deficit approximately in half, not eliminate borrowing. It applies exactly the same percentage surcharge to eligible federal tax liabilities and haircut to all noninterest federal spending. Interest on Treasury debt is exempt. The adjustment phases in over three years, stays in place until debt held by the public falls to 90% of GDP, and then phases out over three years.

How the uniform percentage is calculated

Uniform rate = Half the baseline deficit ÷ (eligible federal receipts + noninterest federal spending)

The same percentage applies on both sides, but the dollar contributions differ because the bases differ. Budget estimates and legal implementation would need to define the eligible tax and payment bases precisely.

At full implementation, the illustrative surcharge raises about $462 billion and the haircut saves about $538 billion annually, before economic feedback.

Three-year phase-in and phase-out

Introduce both adjustments together over 36 months, increasing from zero to the full required percentage. Maintain them while public debt remains above 90% of GDP. Once debt held by the public reaches 90% of GDP, phase both adjustments out evenly over the following 36 months. A practical law should define how to handle subsequent threshold crossings during the phase-out.

Automatic real-GDP recession protection

ConditionAutomatic adjustment
Normal economic conditionsFull scheduled surcharge and haircut
Two consecutive quarters of declining real GDPBoth rates reduced by 50%
Real GDP declines at least 3% over four quartersBoth rates suspended (0%)
Two consecutive quarters of real GDP growthBoth rates gradually restored over 12 months

The rule uses first-published GDP estimates, without retroactively reversing an adjustment when data are revised. No vote or discretionary recession declaration is required. The 90% debt target remains in place during temporary relief.

Why interest payments are exempt

Treasury obligations must be honored. Cutting contractual interest payments risks undermining confidence in U.S. credit and raising future borrowing costs. Treasury principal obligations must also be honored, although principal refinancing is not generally treated as ordinary budget spending.

Implementation challenges

Congress would need to authorize changes to statutory benefits, federal contracts, compensation, taxes, and transfers. Applying a uniform haircut to all noninterest spending has potentially large distributional and operational consequences. The formula is a policy proposal, not an enacted rule or an official CBO estimate.

The principle

One percentage. Shared responsibility. A clear goal.

The objective is to reduce persistent borrowing and leave future generations a more manageable debt burden, while allowing automatic relief during recessions.
